Ending repeat Severity 1 SLA breaches for a Fortune 500 client

Critical outages kept running past a 6-hour SLA because no one owned getting parts and a technician to the same site at the same time. I found the gap, designed the response process that closed it, and the misses stopped within six weeks.

The problem

A site that can't do business can't wait until tomorrow

For this client, any outage that stopped a location from processing sales was a Severity 1 incident with a 6-hour SLA. Every hour past that window was an hour the location could not take a single sale.

This was not an occasional miss. We were consistently breaching the SLA. Repairs routinely ran well past six hours, often overnight or into the next business day. For a Fortune 500 client, a pattern like that puts the whole relationship at risk, not just one ticket.


What I found

The repair wasn't the slow part. The coordination was.

Replacement parts and technicians were dispatched on separate tracks. A technician would arrive with no part, or the part would arrive with no one there to install it, and the site waited for the missing piece. Nobody owned the whole incident from start to finish, so there was no single place where parts, technicians, support and the client came together.

That meant faster troubleshooting alone would not fix the SLA. The process needed one owner and one shared line of communication for every Severity 1.

Before and after

One bridge instead of separate tracks

The 6-hour clock starts the moment the site goes down. Here is what happened next, before and after the redesign.

Before

  1. 1 Site goes down. Severity 1 opens and the 6-hour clock starts.
  2. 2 Technician troubleshoots and confirms hardware is needed.
  3. 3
    Part ordered

    Technician dispatched

    Separate tracks, no owner, no shared timeline

  4. 4 One arrives without the other. The site waits.
  5. 5 Repair finishes past SLA, often overnight or the next day.

After

  1. 1 Site goes down. Severity 1 opens and the 6-hour clock starts.
  2. 2 The on-duty Outage Team opens one bridge, with the client on the line and a manager driving it.
  3. 3 Part and onsite technician are dispatched at the same time, and both are tracked on the bridge until they arrive together.
  4. 4 CSMs send hourly updates: current work, next steps, expected completion.
  5. 5 The original technician verifies with the onsite technician, who stays until every system works.
  6. 6 Reporting's timeline goes to the client and becomes the root cause analysis.

What I designed

The Outage Team bridge

I designed a bridge structure that put everyone needed to close a Severity 1 on one call, each with a clear job:

  • Manager on duty drove the bridge to completion and kept it on task. The role rotated weekly across leadership, including Tier 2 and Tier 3 managers, the Change Management manager, the Project Manager lead and the Director.
  • Original technician who took the call or ticket stayed with the incident and worked directly with the onsite technician, so no history was lost in a handoff.
  • Onsite technician was dispatched immediately, alongside the part, to do the hands-on repair.
  • Partner Engagement team member dispatched the replacement part and stayed on the bridge until it was onsite with the technician.
  • Two CSMs, the on-call CSM for the week and the CSM over the affected account, communicated every update and finding to the client through hourly updates.
  • Reporting team member kept a detailed timeline and notes, sent to the client when the bridge closed and used to build the root cause analysis.

The client and their stakeholders stayed on the bridge too, so they heard progress first-hand instead of waiting for a summary. Because managers and CSMs were in a shared weekly rotation across every account, any outage got the same level of support no matter who was on duty.

Supporting pieces

Safety stock

Worked with Logistics to keep extra switches, access points and cabling on hand, so every client site could receive parts within the SLA window.

Hourly update template

Wrote the template CSMs sent every hour: what was being worked on, next steps and an expected completion window.

Release rule

The onsite technician was not released until the team confirmed every system was working, so an incident only closed once the site was fully back in business.

RCA template

Wrote the root cause analysis template the team completed after each incident and reviewed with the client to find remaining pain points.

Mock outage calls

Organized practice bridges so the team knew how to open a bridge, who to pull in, and when and how documentation went out before a real outage hit.

Results

SLA misses stopped within six weeks

Six weeks after launch, we were no longer missing Severity 1 SLAs for the client, after a period of consistent breaches. Because the process fixed coordination rather than one account's quirks, it was rolled out across every enterprise account on the program, with the shared rotation keeping support consistent for all of them.

What this shows

When a deadline keeps slipping, the delay is often in the handoffs between teams, not in the work itself. Giving the incident one owner and one line of communication fixed what faster troubleshooting alone could not.
