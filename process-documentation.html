Case study  ·  Linden Lab

Rebuilding the team's process guide so new hires could learn the job from it

When our team lead left and two new hires joined, our process guide was out of date in places and missing whole parts of our daily work. I rebuilt it into a guide covering every account we support, organized in the order a new team member needs to learn it. The team now works from it every day.

The problem

The guide no longer matched the work

Our process guide was written by our former team lead, back when the two of us ran every account. We never had time to keep it current. One account's documentation dated from when we first took it over, one to two years earlier, and we had completely rebuilt that account's tools since. None of those steps were accurate anymore.

Whole parts of our daily work had no documentation at all: how we use our team's communication server, our reminder system, our tracking sheets, and our alerts. A lot of what the team needed to know lived only in my head. Updating the guide had sat on our to-do list for a long time. When our team lead left and we hired two new people, who I would be training, my manager and I both wanted it done.

What I found

Out of date, incomplete, and built for reference instead of learning

The old guide's individual steps were clearly written. The problem was everything around them. It opened with account details and policy documents, so a new hire had to read through reference material before learning how the team actually works. Shared processes were repeated for each account, so a single change meant editing several places. Each account section was organized a little differently, so knowing one didn't help you find your way around another.

Fixing individual steps wouldn't have been enough. The guide needed a new structure built around how people learn the job.

What I designed

Design choices

Built in onboarding order

The guide starts with how the team works day to day, then moves to processes, then account details. A new hire can read it front to back and be productive, while experienced team members can jump straight to what they need.

Documented what was missing

Wrote new sections for the tools and workflows the team used every day but had never written down, including the knowledge that lived only in my head.

Written once, tagged by account

Shared processes appear once, with color-coded callouts showing which accounts each step applies to. Differences between accounts are easy to spot, and a change only needs to be made in one place.

One template for every account

Every account section follows the same order of subsections. Once someone learns one account, they know where to find everything in the others.

Shown, not just told

Added more than 90 screenshots, many marked up to point to the exact button, field or setting a step refers to.

Linked, not repeated

Related sections link to each other instead of restating the same steps, so the guide stays shorter and stays accurate.

How I built it

Two to three weeks of writing, one week of testing

I spent two to three weeks rewriting the guide across every account. I kept what was still accurate from the old guide, corrected what had changed, and wrote the missing sections from my own day-to-day work. That included documenting processes I had redesigned myself, like our automated follow-up notices.

Before releasing it, I asked my new teammates and my manager for thorough testing and feedback, and spent about a week on revisions. I still update it regularly as our processes change.

Results

The guide the team works from every day

Our two new hires onboarded and trained with the new guide, which made their onboarding faster and gave training a clear structure to follow. The whole team now uses it every day as the standard for how we work, and we make far fewer mistakes than we did before it existed. It's a living document that I keep current as our work changes.

What this shows

Documentation goes out of date quietly, one changed step at a time, until the guide describes a team that no longer exists. Keeping it useful means more than fixing steps: it has to match the real work and be organized the way people actually learn it.
